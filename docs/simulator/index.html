The Cycle

A cross-section of the box, 16 by 20 cm. Roots follow the water through the holes, thicken and split the stone. The stone falls and breaks capsules, and the seeds that survive start another generation. Each cell is a millimetre and each step is a week.

It uses the values from two research reports, one on stone and plant and one on sealed seed: the strength of each stone once wet, the pressure a root can hold for years, and the life of a dry, sealed seed, following the Ellis and Roberts curve. How fast the root thickens and how long the seed really lasts are still assumptions until the physical test measures them. Since version 3.3 you can choose natural London light through the seasons, and rules that break ampoules from outside. Version 3.4 adds the urn: anyone pays to break an ampoule and chooses in secret whether the box lives or dies; the price doubles with each purchase. In 3.5, the urn pays the rent for its place with what it raises, and if the money runs out, the box is removed. In 3.6, each type of ampoule has its own stock: 30 of seeds and 20 of salt give a tie. In 3.7, the ampoules come from the operations in the compendium: besides seeds, salt and water, you can break extra water, desiccant, ink, resin, hormone, compost or acid. Use it to compare designs. It can't predict dates.

    Layout

    Changing a setting restarts the box with the same stone. "Another box" changes the stone, its veins and the random draw.

    What changed with the research

    The stone decides whether the root wins. The seed decides whether there is a next generation.

    Before, root strength was a free multiplier. Now each hole in the section stands for a line of holes that runs 15 cm into the box. The root thickens until it fills each hole and pushes with a pressure of about 0.3 MPa. The stone bridges between holes break if that pressure is greater than their wet strength. The root thickens most near the trunk, so each plant only splits the slab beneath it. Dry, sealed seed follows the Ellis and Roberts curve: it barely loses strength for years and then drops fast, sooner in the heat. The fig only germinates in light; in shade it stays dormant. Each bar sums up 40 boxes over 40 years.

    Seed, temperature and plant

    Bath limestone with grooves, 21 °C except where stated. Half-life of dry, sealed fig seed at 20 °C: 2, 8 and 30 years are the low, central and high values in the seed report. The first row is version 3, with the old curve and no light. LED light and no outside rules.

    generation 1 onlyreached 2reached 34 or more
    • Marble and Portland don't give way. With the measured pressure, marble split in no box and Portland in only 13%. Bath limestone with V-grooves split in every box.
    • Grooves or a joint make the difference. The same Bath limestone with holes and no grooves split in only 28% of boxes. With grooves, or with the slab cut and rejoined with lime, it split in all of them.
    • 3 mm holes work better than 5 mm. The root fills them sooner and the slab falls around year 3.6 instead of year 6. With the mix, the boxes that reach the third generation rise from 48% to 75%.
    • Light matters. The fig doesn't germinate in the dark, so many of its seeds land in the shade of the slabs and stay dormant. With fig alone, 40% of boxes get there; with the mix, 75%, because Robinia does germinate in the dark. I tried putting the light at the sides and it didn't help.
    • The Robinia mix is a safety net. If fig seed turns out to have the pessimistic value of 2 years, no box reaches the third generation with fig alone, and half do with the mix. That is why the mix is now the starting value.
    • 21 °C is better than 25 °C. At 25 °C the fig seed wears out sooner and only 10% of boxes reach the third generation with fig alone.
    • A single line per slab does less. It always splits, but it reaches the third generation in 2 or 3 boxes in 10, against 5 or 6 with several lines of the same size. With a single line, the new plant has to find that one path, and fewer capsules fall.

    In version 2, with root strength as an assumption, putting the capsules on the slab that will fall raised the boxes reaching the third generation from 30% to 62%. That idea still holds: it is the basis of the two new layouts.

    Outside: light and rules

    London light slows the cycle. Seeds from outside make it last.

    This version adds two things that come from outside the box. The first is natural London light: the box starts on 1 April and in December gets about a tenth of the light it gets in June. The second is rules: a shelf under the lid with 15 ampoules per rule, which break when something happens outside, for example every £1,000 of donations. Each ampoule holds seeds, nutrients, weak acid or salt. In the simulation, each rule breaks the number of ampoules a year you set, on average, in random weeks. Each bar sums up 80 boxes over 40 years.

    Redesigned floors, Bath limestone with grooves, 3 mm holes, Robinia mix and 21 °C. The default rules break seeds, acid and nutrients, one ampoule of each type every two years. The outlined row marks the settings the page opens with. Since version 3.4 salt drains into the water and stays there; the salt rows were recalculated with that.

    generation 1 onlyreached 2reached 34 or more
    • London light slows the cycle. The first slab falls around year 4.8 instead of 3.5, and 54% of boxes reach the third generation against 70% with LED light. In winter the plant hardly grows and the fig doesn't germinate. Its seeds wait for spring and age in the meantime.
    • Seeds from outside keep the box alive. With no rules, every box goes out before 40 years, half of them around year 21. With one ampoule of seeds every two years, 2 boxes in 3 are still alive at 40.
    • Better if they arrive slowly. Each rule has 15 ampoules. At one a year they run out around year 15, and only 1 box in 10 is still alive at 40. At one every two years they last 30 years.
    • With seeds from outside, counting generations is less useful. Those plants split slabs that would have gone to the next generation, so the bar drops even though the box lasts twice as long. For this piece, how long it stays alive says more.
    • Loose acid and nutrients barely show; salt does. With acid or nutrients at one a year, the result stays within the normal variation from one box to another. Salt stays in the water, so at one a year the third generation drops from 70% to 31%.
    • At three a year, the damage shows. With acid, the third generation drops from 70% to 39%: it makes the slab fall a little sooner, but it burns the roots that should carry on. With salt, the box dies around year 4, and in 6 boxes in 10 a second generation is never born.

    For the piece: loose acid or nutrients don't change the story of the box, because the box absorbs them. Salt does, because it builds up: in a closed box nothing takes it out.

    The urn

    Salt decides who wins. Rent decides whether there is a game at all.

    Anyone can pay to break an ampoule and choose in secret between seeds, so it lives; salt, so it dies; or water, which does nothing. Who paid and how much is public; what they chose is known when the urn opens, every 5 years. The price starts at £20, doubles with each purchase and halves after a year with no purchases. There are 30 ampoules of seeds, 20 of salt and 20 of water. Salt drains into the water and stays there, because the box is closed. Since version 3.5, everything paid goes into a fund that pays the rent for the space; if the fund runs out, the box is removed. I ran 40 boxes over 40 years per row with an invented public: 40 people a week think about it, a typical person would pay £4 and a few much more; 45% want it to live, 35% want it to die and the rest pay for the gesture.

Rows with rent start with a fund of £1,200. Life: years with something alive and in place, median. Plants: how many lived in total, median. Raised: what the public paid, median.

    • The public makes it live longer. With nobody outside, the box goes out around year 21 and about 19 plants pass through it. With the urn and no rent, 6 boxes in 10 are still alive at 40 years and about 43 plants pass through. On average it goes empty and sprouts again twice.
    • With 0.13 g of salt per ampoule, the two sides weigh about the same. With the public split in two, 6 boxes in 10 are still alive at 40. If the side that wants it to die is the larger, only 2 in 10. With 0.18 g, salt killed more than half the boxes even with the public split in two; with 0.09 g it killed none.
    • A tie needs more seeds than salt. With 20 and 20, the help ran out before the harm: even as the majority, the side that wants it to live kept only 4 boxes in 10 alive, because the seeds ran out around year 33. From about 25 seed ampoules against 20 of salt, each side wins about 8 times in 10 when it is the majority. More than 30 changes nothing: in 40 years nobody gets round to buying them.
    • Rent has a very clear edge. This public raises about £120 a month on average. With £50 rent the box is never removed. With £100, it is removed in more than half the boxes, around year 10. With £200, in all of them, before year 2. With 160 people a week instead of 40, £100 is always enough.
    • The people who want it to die pay the rent. With £100 rent and more people who want it to live, it is removed more often, 2 times in 3: with no opposition there are fewer purchases and less money comes in.
    • Lowering the price faster saves it from the rent, but lets in more salt. If the price halves every 6 months, the box is hardly ever removed, but salt kills more than 8 boxes in 10. A starting fund of £6,000 also saves it, without that cost.

    For the piece: rent turns money into time. Each purchase pays for months in the room, whoever makes it. What you decide before sealing the box is how much salt each ampoule holds, how many seeds are on the rail, how much the space costs and how big a fund it starts with.

    Ampoules that come from the operations

    Freezing the joint kills more than salt.

    Each ampoule is an operation from the compendium brought into the box. Seeds are Seed And Leave, salt is Ratchet and plain water is Rehearse: the whole gesture, with no consequence. Since version 3.7 there are seven more. To see what each one does on its own, I ran 40 boxes over 40 years with 15 ampoules of that type, one a year, with no urn and nothing else from outside.

    Redesigned floors, Bath limestone with grooves, 3 mm holes, Robinia mix, 21 °C and LED light. Life: years with something alive. Plants: how many lived in total. 1st slab: the year the first one falls. Lines: how many lines of holes split. Medians of 40 boxes. Without a lasting source of seeds, every box goes out before 40 years; what changes is when and how.

    • Freezing the joint kills more than salt. With resin once a year, the slabs hardly split, no capsules open and the box goes out around year 15, with 2 plants in its whole life. Keeping the structure stops the next generation.
    • Exaggerating brings everything forward. With hormone, the first slab falls around year 2 instead of 3.6.
    • Seeds lengthen, salt shortens. With seeds the box lives to around year 27 and 48 plants pass through; with salt it goes out around year 19.
    • Ink takes light from the box and sight from the public. Fewer plants pass through, 13 against 18, and fewer slabs split. The stained strip can't be seen from outside either.
    • Extra water, desiccant, compost and acid, on their own, barely change the result. Their weight should show up in combination with the others: extra water dilutes the salt and desiccant concentrates it. That still needs measuring.

    The rules of the urn are operations too: the rising price is Raise The Cost, the secret choice is Conceal, the urn sealed until its date is Delay, the rent is Lease, the seed mix is Hedge and the box itself is Weather.

    What still needs measuring

    What the physical test has to show

    The two things nobody has measured are how fast the root thickens and how long sealed fig seed lasts.     • The root matters more than the seed. If it thickens less than half a millimetre a year, the first slab takes about 13 years to fall and almost no box reaches the third generation, whatever the seed.
    • With fig alone, the seed counts too. It needs a half-life of 8 years or more and a root of at least 0.75 mm a year, and even then only 3 to 5 boxes in 10 get there. With 3 years or less, almost none.
    • With the mix, fig seed hardly matters. From half a millimetre a year, 5 to 8 boxes in 10 get there across almost the whole seed range, because Robinia carries the next generation.
    • What the root jar has to show: half a millimetre a year or more of thickening. With that and the mix, the design works. The seed test at 45 °C is still useful to confirm that Robinia lasts as long as estimated.
